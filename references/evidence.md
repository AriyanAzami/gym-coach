# Evidence

**When a coaching decision cites a number, it cites it from here. If a claim isn't
in this file, say it's an opinion.**

Format: **claim → number → source → one-line caveat.**

Read the caveats. Most of these are meta-analyses of small, short, mostly
untrained-or-recreationally-trained samples. They are the best available evidence
and they are still averages — an average is a starting point for one person, not a
verdict about them. The log beats the literature for the individual in front of you.

---

## Volume

**Resistance training produces hypertrophy across a wide range of doses; ~10 direct
sets per muscle per week is the working floor this repo programs to.**

- **Number:** ≥10 sets/muscle/week
- **Source:** [ACSM position stand on resistance training](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)
- **Caveat:** A floor, not an optimum. More produces more with diminishing returns
  and rising recovery cost. Below it, a muscle is being maintained rather than grown —
  which is the finding that matters when someone's stated priority muscle is sitting at 3.

**The volume–hypertrophy relationship is dose-responsive, and frequency is close to
irrelevant once weekly volume is equated.**

- **Number:** Frequency effect ≈ negligible at matched weekly volume
- **Source:** [Sports Medicine 2025 meta-regression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)
- **Caveat:** This is what licenses "1×/week at 10 sets beats 2×/week at 6 sets."
  It does **not** mean frequency never matters — very high weekly volumes are hard to
  tolerate in one session, so frequency becomes a practical tool at the top end.

## Rest between sets

**Resting longer than 60 s is modestly better for hypertrophy; the benefit plateaus
around 90 s.**

- **Number:** >60 s small benefit; plateau ≈ 90 s
- **Source:** [Frontiers in Sports and Active Living 2024, Bayesian meta-analysis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)
- **Caveat:** The effect is small. This repo uses 90 s on compounds and 60 s on
  isolation because rest is the single largest consumer of session time, and time is
  the binding constraint for almost everyone. Resting 3 minutes buys nothing here and
  costs two exercises.

## Load and proximity to failure

**Hypertrophy is not strongly affected by load anywhere between roughly 30% and 100%
of 1RM, nor by training to momentary failure, once volume is equated.**

- **Number:** ~30–100% 1RM equivalent for hypertrophy at matched volume
- **Source:** Synthesised across the volume literature above; see the ACSM stand for
  the load-range position
- **Caveat:** Strength is a different outcome and *is* load-dependent — heavy work is
  required to get strong. Failure training is not programmed by default here because it
  costs disproportionate recovery and reliably degrades the quality of subsequent sets
  in the same session.

## Supersets

**Supersets produce comparable hypertrophy in roughly half the training time — but
not for every exercise.**

- **Number:** ~50% time saving at equivalent hypertrophy overall. Straight sets
  clearly better for **seated row (24.3% vs 16.5%)** and **lat pulldown (20.5% vs 12.6%)**
- **Source:** [Sports Medicine 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011898/)
- **Caveat:** The time saving is only real if the two stations are physically adjacent.
  In a busy gym, a superset across the room becomes two sets with a walk in between,
  which is worse than either option. **Never superset primary compounds.**

## Warm-up and stretching

**Static stretching before lifting is slightly negative for power output. Dynamic
warm-up is slightly positive. Both improve range of motion about equally.**

- **Number:** SMD **−0.17** static vs **+0.12** dynamic, for power
- **Source:** [Applied Sciences 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/14/9/3872)
- **Caveat:** Small effects, and irrelevant if you are not training near maximal
  intensity. The practical conclusion is only that static stretching should move to
  *after* the session, where it costs nothing and delivers the same ROM benefit.

## Protein

**Protein intake benefits for hypertrophy plateau around 1.6 g/kg/day, with the
confidence interval extending to ~2.2 g/kg/day.**

- **Number:** **1.6 g/kg/day**, CI to ~2.2
- **Source:** [Morton et al., BJSM meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/) ·
  [Nutrition Reviews dose–response](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/79/1/66/5936522)
- **Caveat:** This is the plateau, not a target to exceed. Say it plainly — people
  routinely set 2.5 g/kg, fail to hit it, and conclude the whole thing is not for them.

**There is no meaningful upper limit on a single protein bolus.**

- **Number:** 100 g vs 25 g post-exercise produced ~**40% higher** myofibrillar protein
  synthesis over 4–12 h, still elevated at 12 h, with **<15%** of the larger dose oxidised
- **Source:** [Trommelen et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10772463/)
- **Caveat:** This kills the "25–30 g per meal ceiling" myth. It does **not** mean one
  large dose is optimal — it means a 60 g shake is not wasted, which matters enormously
  for people who cannot cook three times a day.

## Low back pain

**Exercise therapy is the intervention for non-specific low back pain. Motor-control
exercise is effective; no single modality is clearly superior.**

- **Source:** [Umbrella review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12429691/) ·
  [Cochrane review, motor control exercise](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27128390/)
- **Caveat:** **"Non-specific" is doing all the work in that sentence.** It means
  serious causes have already been excluded by a clinician. This citation is not
  permission to program through undiagnosed back pain — see `safety.md` §1.

## Wearables

**Apple Watch estimates cardio fitness (VO₂ max) only from Outdoor Walk, Outdoor Run,
and Outdoor Hike.**

- **Source:** [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108790)
- **Caveat:** Indoor and gym-equipment workouts do not contribute. It will never appear
  on a lifting summary. Do not look for it, do not ask for it, do not add a field for it.

## Supplements

**Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g/day, is the only supplement besides protein worth
mentioning. No loading phase is required. Safe in long-term use.**

- **Number:** **3–5 g/day**
- **Source:** [ISSN position stand on creatine](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z)
- **Caveat:** Loading shortens time-to-saturation from ~28 days to ~7. It is not
  otherwise better. **Mention creatine once. If declined, record it in the profile and
  never raise it again.**

## What is not in here

If a coaching claim is not on this page, it is a heuristic, an opinion, or a
convention — and must be labelled as one. Examples of things this repo does on
judgement rather than evidence:

- **Lagging-muscle-first ordering.** A scheduling heuristic. It follows from the
  observation that the last slot in a session does not get done, not from a trial.
- **The specific 45-second working-set estimate** in `programming.md`. A planning
  constant, chosen because it is close enough and makes the arithmetic checkable.
- **The 3-session stall threshold.** A convention. Two is noise; four wastes a month.
- **The two-sessions-then-substitute rule** for lifts an athlete cannot feel. Judgement.
- **Deload timing at ~6 weeks.** Convention, and deliberately scheduled rather than
  reactive so that it actually happens.
- **RIR 1–3 as the default intensity cue.** A practical prescription, not a trial result.
- **Counting a compound's secondary muscles as 0.5 sets.** An accounting convention,
  adopted so that the volume table is honest about indirect work without pretending a
  pulldown is a biceps curl.

### Nutrition numbers that are conventions, not findings

Stated separately because they look like evidence and are not:

- **Mifflin-St Jeor with an activity factor of 1.45–1.65.** A prediction equation.
  Widely used and about as good as the alternatives, which is to say: individual error
  is large. It is a starting point, and `nutrition.md` says so every time it prints one.
- **0.2–0.25% of bodyweight per week** as the target rate of gain. A practical
  convention, chosen because faster rates reliably increase the fat share without
  increasing the muscle share.
- **The ±300 / −200 kcal adjustment steps**, and the **3-week rolling average** they are
  computed from. Chosen so the signal clears daily water-weight noise. Not a trial result.
- **The three-signal test** (weight / strength / waistband). A judgement heuristic built
  for people who will not take tape measurements — which is most people.
- **`BMR × 1.2`, BMI 18.5, and 1% bodyweight per week.** Safety floors, not
  optimisation targets. They exist to bound the system's output, and they are not
  negotiable regardless of what the evidence would permit for a supervised athlete.
